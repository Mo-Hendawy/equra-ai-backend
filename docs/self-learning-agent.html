Self-Learning AI Agents: How AI Agents Learn from Mistakes

Most AI agents forget everything between runs. Ours remembers its mistakes, challenges its own logic, scores its predictions against reality, and rewrites its own strategy every week. Here's exactly how it works.

M
Mohamed Mohab 10 min read · Apr 2026

We built an AI agent that analyzes stocks on the Egyptian Exchange. It gives you a recommendation — Buy, Hold, Sell — with a fair value, confidence score, and reasoning.

The first version was stateless. Every run started cold. It recommended Buy on CIB at 62 EGP, lost 8%, and the next time it saw the same setup it said Buy again. Same reasoning. Same mistake. It didn't know it was wrong because it couldn't remember being wrong.

So we rebuilt it with a self-learning loop. Not "self-learning" in the marketing sense — not fine-tuning, not reinforcement learning. A concrete, auditable system where every recommendation is logged, scored against reality, and fed back into the next analysis.

This article walks through exactly how it works, using one real stock — Commercial International Bank (COMI) on the EGX — as the running example.

. . .

The Loop

The self-learning loop has six stages. Each one feeds the next.

Let's walk through each stage with a real example.

. . .

Stage 1: Remember

Before every analysis, the agent queries its memory. Not a cache — a persistent SQLite database that stores every recommendation it has ever made, every lesson it has learned, and the current strategy it operates under.

Three things happen before the LLM sees a single token:

1. Check past decisions for this stock. "I recommended Buy on COMI 12 days ago at 82.5 EGP. It's now 85.1 EGP (+3.1%). That call is tracking well."

2. Retrieve similar episodes. The agent searches a vector database for past situations that look like the current one — not by stock name, but by context. "Last time I saw a bank stock with P/E below 7 during a CBE rate cut cycle and volume 30% below average, momentum signals were wrong by 8%." That episode gets injected into the prompt.

3. Load the active strategy. The strategy is a versioned document that the Meta-Agent rewrites weekly. It contains rules like: "Discount RSI crossovers when volume is below 50% of the 30-day average." The analysis model reads this strategy before generating its recommendation.

Why this matters: Without memory, every run starts from zero. The agent makes the same mistake twice because it doesn't know it was wrong. Episodic memory turns individual failures into reusable lessons. According to Phil Schmid's research on agent memory, "the consequences of a flawed memory strategy only surface over time" — which is why most teams skip it and regret it later.
. . .

Stage 2: Analyze

The analysis runs through Gemini with three layers of context injected into the prompt:

The model sees the strategy, the lessons from similar past situations, and the raw stock data — all before generating a single word. This is not a generic prompt. It's a prompt that contains the agent's accumulated experience.

The output is a structured JSON: recommendation, confidence, fair value estimate, reasoning steps, risk signals, and price targets.

. . .

Stage 3: Challenge

Before the recommendation ships, a second agent attacks it. The Critic Agent's only job is to find the single strongest reason the recommendation is wrong.

This is not a balanced counterpoint. It's adversarial. If the Analysis Agent says Buy, the Critic is forced to argue Sell first — then judge whether its own counter-argument is strong enough to downgrade confidence.

Critic Agent Output — COMI Buy Recommendation
{ "counterRecommendation": "Sell", "weakness": "The analysis overlooks the impact of recent EGP devaluations on COMI's asset quality and real earnings. The stated 'low P/E' may be misleading due to stale FRA data and unaddressed credit risk from high NPL formation in a sustained high-interest-rate environment.", "severity": "high", "counterScenario": "A 30-40% downward revision of future EPS estimates driven by rising non-performing loans, pushing the forward P/E significantly higher and eliminating the perceived undervaluation.", "blockingIssues": [ "Stale earnings risk — FRA disclosure lag", "Unaddressed asset quality deterioration", "EGP devaluation impact on real returns" ] }

The Critic found a high-severity issue: the earnings data might be stale due to FRA filing lag, and the P/E ratio could be misleading. This is an EGX-specific risk that a generic AI would miss.

Because severity is "high," the confidence gets automatically discounted. The Buy recommendation still ships — but with reduced confidence and the Critic's objections visible in the output.

Research backs this up. An adversarial self-critique study in insurance underwriting found that adding a Critic Agent improved decision accuracy from 92% to 96% and reduced hallucinations from 11.3% to 3.8%. The trade-off is ~33% latency overhead — worth it for financial recommendations.

. . .

Stage 5: Score

Every day at 15:30 Cairo time (after EGX market close), a cron job runs. It looks at every recommendation older than 5 trading days that hasn't been scored yet, fetches the current price, and computes the return.

5-Day Scoring — COMI
Decision #47: Buy COMI at 122 EGP on Apr 8 Current price: 126.5 EGP (Apr 15) Return: +3.7% Direction: Correct (Buy + positive return) Verdict: ✓ Good call
5-Day Scoring — HRHO
Decision #43: Buy HRHO at 14.2 EGP on Apr 3 Current price: 12.8 EGP (Apr 10) Return: -9.9% Direction: WRONG (Buy + negative return > 5%) Verdict: ✗ Bad call — auto-generating episode

Three scoring windows run at different intervals:

WindowScheduleWhat It Tells You
5-dayDaily 15:30 CairoWas I immediately right or wrong?
30-dayEvery Friday 16:00Did my thesis play out?

When a bad outcome is detected (>5% wrong direction), the system automatically creates an episode — a lesson learned — and stores it in both SQLite and a vector database for future retrieval.

The episode for HRHO would be: "5d outcome: -9.9% — recommendation was Buy but price dropped. Volume was 30% below 30-day average at time of recommendation. Review thesis assumptions for low-volume momentum setups."

Next time the agent sees a similar low-volume setup, that episode gets injected into the prompt. The agent literally reads its own past mistake before making the same type of call.

. . .

Stage 6: Evolve

Every Sunday at 17:00 Cairo time, the Meta-Agent runs. It's a separate AI call (Gemini Flash, not the primary analysis model) that reviews the last 30 decisions and their outcomes.

The Meta-Agent doesn't just log what went wrong. It rewrites the strategy. The new version replaces the old one, and every subsequent analysis reads the updated rules.

Here's what happens inside the Meta-Agent every Sunday at 17:00 Cairo time:

The strategy is versioned. You can diff v4 against v5 and see exactly what changed and why. You can roll it back if the new version performs worse. There's an audit trail for every evolution.

This is not fine-tuning. The model weights never change. What changes is the strategy prompt — the set of rules the model follows. This is cheaper, faster, and fully auditable. You can see what changed, why it changed, and undo it in one API call.

The Poison Guard

The hardest part of self-learning isn't building the loop. It's preventing the loop from learning the wrong lessons.

Example: the agent recommends Buy on COMI based on strong P/E analysis. A week later, CBE unexpectedly raises rates. COMI drops 12%. The 5-day scoring marks it as a bad call.

But was it? The thesis was correct — the P/E was genuinely undervalued. The macro shock was unpredictable. If the Meta-Agent learns "avoid P/E-based bank analysis," it's learning the wrong lesson.

This is why every decision has an invalidation_reason field:

ReasonFeeds Learning?Example
THESIS_ERRORYesAnalysis was wrong — bad math, missed data
MACRO_SHOCKNoCBE rate hike, political crisis, FX crash
DATA_STALENoEarnings were 60 days old (FRA lag)
TIMINGNoThesis was right, entry timing was wrong

Only THESIS_ERROR decisions feed the Meta-Agent. Everything else is logged but excluded from the learning loop. The agent learns from its own mistakes, not from market chaos.
